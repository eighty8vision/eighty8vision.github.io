eighty8 vision is for women entrepreneurs.

Business ownership that leverages your energy, reflects your potential, and supports the life you actually want to create.

Whether you are early in building your business or already established and navigating growth and scale, eighty8 vision provides a methodology and roadmap that repositions the owner and the business, determines its operating reality, and creates clarity, focus, and prioritization.

Women-owned businesses are increasing in number but declining in their share of total revenue. In 2025, women owned 40.6% of U.S. businesses, yet generated only 4.6% of total revenue, down from 6.2% the year prior.

Between 2022 and 2025, women-owned businesses grew 12.1%, nearly twice the growth rate of men-owned businesses (6.3%). Yet, only 9% of women-owned businesses have employees, compared with 18% of men-owned businesses. Those 9% of employer firms generate 82% of all women-owned revenue.

+15 years operating inside owner-led businesses, I saw the same pattern repeatedly. Most issues were not strategy, people, execution, or opportunity problems. They were the result of how the business was built to function and how an owner's relationship with the business shapes its trajectory.

Ambition and ability have never been the problem.

The question is not whether women-owned businesses are capable of claiming their proportionate share of the market.

The question is what is actually limiting them.

eighty8 vision is built to diagnose what is actually limiting your business, to see what you can't see from inside it, and to show you what needs to change first.

The Framework

The core principles of eighty8 vision define how owner-led businesses grow, where they become constrained, and what is required for them to evolve.

Most business solutions focus on isolated tactics, surface symptoms, or generic growth strategies.

eighty8 vision starts with the owner. A business is a reflection of how it has been led, how it has been structured, and what stage of development it is currently in.

To assess a business correctly, three factors must be read together:

Owner's Internal Operating System

How the owner relates to the business, thinks, decides, leads, tolerates pressure, views money, handles uncertainty, and considers the broader context, including the life the business is meant to support.

Operational Structure

How the business has been built to function through systems, roles, processes, accountability, capacity, and execution.

Owner's Internal Operating System

What the business is capable of becoming is determined first by an owner's internal operating system. It is a collection of their beliefs, standards, identity attachments, emotional patterns, decision-making habits, fears, tolerance for uncertainty and risk, relationship with money, relationship with control, and the way pressure, growth, responsibility, and success are interpreted.

These internal factors determine how an owner behaves inside the business and how they relate to it over time. Whether conscious or unconscious, they drive repeated behavior and become visible in the business.

They shape what is noticed, what is avoided, what is prioritized, what is delayed, what is tolerated, how quickly decisions are made, how people are led, how problems are handled, and whether the business is built intentionally or reactively.

If the owner lacks clarity, the business reflects confusion. If the owner avoids discomfort, the business reflects avoidance. If the owner tolerates weak standards, the business reflects weak standards. If the owner operates with discipline, accountability, and long-term thinking, the business reflects those conditions as well.

The owner's internal operating system does not just influence performance. It determines how much of the business's available potential can be realized. Opportunities, demand, talent, resources, and favorable conditions only matter to the degree the owner can recognize them, respond to them, structure for them, and sustain them.

Most business constraints are owner constraints expressed through operations.

Operational Structure

Operational structure is the external expression of how a business has been designed to function. It includes systems, processes, workflows, roles and responsibilities, communication channels, documentation, accountability, decision rights, methods of delivery, capacity planning, and the overall architecture through which work gets done.

Operational structure determines how efficiently and effectively a business can operate, how dependent it remains on the owner, how well people inside and outside the business can collaborate, whether growth creates leverage or strain, and how easily the business can transition, or evolve over time.

Strong structure compounds. It reduces friction, preserves energy, creates consistency, improves speed of execution, reduces avoidable issues, enables delegation, increases usable capacity, and supports stable growth. Weak structure also compounds. It creates bottlenecks, confusion, rework, risk, owner dependence, mistakes, missed opportunities, unnecessary stress, and unnecessary costs.

Operational structure is never separate from the owner. It is the accumulated result of the owner's decisions, omissions, standards, priorities, and relationship with the business over time. What is documented or undocumented, delegated or hoarded, measured or ignored, what has been tolerated, and what has been delayed all become visible through structure.

If the business structure is producing stress, chaos, dependence, inefficiency, or stagnation, it is evidence that earlier owner decisions did not match reality, capacity, stage, or desired direction.

Structure should therefore be read diagnostically. It reveals how the owner has been operating and what the business has been prepared, or not prepared, to support.

The goal is not structure for its own sake. The goal is stage-appropriate structure that supports the owner's capacity, desired role, intended life outcomes, and long-term direction.

The 5 Stages of Business Development

Every owner-led business moves through predictable stages of development. These stages are not defined by revenue, headcount, or years in operation. They are defined by what the business requires structurally and what it needs from the owner's relationship with it.

Each stage has its own operational reality. What works in one stage becomes a constraint in the next. The structure that supported early growth becomes the bottleneck that prevents the next level. The owner behaviors that built the business in one stage are often the same behaviors limiting it in the next.

The framework maps each stage across two dimensions simultaneously: the operational structure of the business and the owner's internal operating system as it relates to the business. They are read together because they are interdependent. Structure reflects the owner. The owner's evolution determines what structure becomes possible.

Businesses are typically measured by revenue and number of employees. Most business advice and solutions are designed around those same metrics, which tell an incomplete story.

Revenue does not reveal whether a business is structurally sound, how the owner is operating, or whether the business is prepared to evolve.

Knowing which stage of development your business is in gives you a more accurate and holistic lens for setting priorities, solving problems, and making decisions in sequence.

Within each stage, the framework identifies three conditions:

In Alignment

The behaviors, decisions, and structural choices that allow the stage to function as intended. When both the owner's operating system and the business structure match what the stage requires, the business produces results proportional to the effort and resources going into it.

Out of Alignment

The behaviors, decisions, and structural choices that restrict what the stage can produce. These are often invisible to the owner because they feel like normal operating conditions. Overwhelm feels like the cost of entrepreneurship. Control feels like competence. Doing everything feels like dedication. They are the owner's internal operating system expressing itself through the business in ways that limit capacity, growth, and sustainability.

Next Stage Readiness

The specific conditions, both structural and owner-level, that must be in place before the business can successfully move into the next stage of development. Attempting to advance without these conditions produces instability, regression, or growth that cannot sustain itself.

The stages are sequential but progression is not the goal. Each stage is neutral. A business can operate effectively at any stage indefinitely if the structure and the owner's relationship with it match what that stage requires. The goal is not to reach Stage 5. The goal is for the business to be operating at the stage that supports the life the owner wants to live.

Without stage context, an owner may invest energy in later-stage solutions while still facing earlier-stage constraints.

5 Stages of Business Development

Every stage is defined by two components: how the business is structured and how the owner is operating inside it.

Within each stage, you will see which behaviors and structures are in alignment, which are out of alignment, and what must be in place before moving forward.

What works in one stage becomes a constraint in the next. Most issues arise when the business or the owner is operating in a way that no longer matches what the stage requires.

Progression is not the goal. Each stage is neutral. What matters is whether the business is structured to support the life you want.

As you read, identify where your business is operating and what that stage requires.

Stage One
You Are the Business

The owner is every function, every decision, every process. The business operates at the speed of the owner and only at the speed of the owner. Patterns set here become the foundation everything else is built on.

Stage Two
You Are the Bottleneck

The business has outgrown what one person can sustain. Revenue is consistent enough to prove the concept but the owner's capacity is fully consumed by maintaining what exists. The ceiling is no longer the market. The ceiling is the owner.

Stage Three
You Operate the Business

Collaboration is necessary. The owner still plays a central role in executing the day-to-day work while also managing the people doing it. Leadership identity, delegation, and accountability become the primary developmental challenges. The decisions being made now carry higher stakes and begin to lock in how the business operates internally and presents itself externally.

Stage Four
The Business Operates Without You

The business has departments, systems, specialties, and processes that function independently of the owner's daily involvement. The owner's role shifts from doing the work to leading the people, the business, and the increasing complexity that comes with scale.

Stage Five
The Business Evolves Without You

The business has its own trajectory, identity, and momentum independent of the owner. The owner's relationship with the business is one of support and discernment. The questions at this stage are no longer about how to make the business work. They are about what the owner wants her relationship with it to be from here.

Stage 1
You are the business
Operational Structure
  • You are every department and every role. Sales, delivery, operations, finance, marketing, customer service.
  • You hold the entire business in your head. You know where everything is, how it works, and why it is done that way.
  • Few or no documented processes exist because you are the process.
  • The business operates only as fast as you can think, decide, and execute.
  • The volume of decisions is high, and the line between what is consequential and what is trivial is often unclear.
  • Revenue is likely irregular.
  • Client acquisition depends almost entirely on your personal energy and presence.
  • You are spending time on tasks that are necessary but outside your strengths or interests.
  • The behavioral and structural patterns you set now will be inherited at every stage that follows.
Owner Relationship
  • Your mood is the business's mood. Your energy is its energy.
  • Your identity is fused with the business.
  • The volume of decisions and the inability to distinguish the consequential from the trivial creates a baseline pressure you stop noticing because it is constant.
  • Wonder and overwhelm coexist as twin responses to constant newness. Both are real, both are present, and they mature together as you gain mastery.
  • Every first matters: your first hard conversation, first boundary, first failure, first unexpected success. Your responses are forming the default leadership instincts that the business will inherit.
Operational Structure
Out of Alignment
  • You are running everything based on what is working in the moment without any consideration for how it would translate to anyone else operating the business.
  • You are skipping documentation because you can hold it in your head, and you are telling yourself you will get to it later when you have time.
  • Pricing was set based on fear, scarcity, or comparison rather than value or sustainability.
  • You are implementing systems or processes without a clear definition of what success actually looks like for you, which means you are building in directions you may later need to reverse.
  • Every shortcut you are taking now is becoming a habit that will be a barrier in later stages. Rework costs escalate exponentially with each stage.
  • The business is operating without any structure that exists outside your head, your calendar, or your memory.
  • You are not capturing the patterns and lessons of this stage because you have not allocated the time to reflect.
In Alignment
  • You are building structure that feels slightly too big for "just you", because you understand that what you set up now becomes the foundation for everything that follows.
  • You are documenting frequently used processes and decisions, even when they feel obvious.
  • You are moving critical information out of your head into a shared, accessible place, even if you are the only one accessing it right now.
  • Pricing reflects value and sustainability, not fear or comparison.
  • You are implementing systems and processes with a clear definition of what success looks like for you, so you are building in the right direction from the start.
  • You are observing where shortcuts are tempting and choosing to invest in structure now rather than rework it later.
  • You are setting aside time, even small amounts, to reflect on what is working, what is not, and what patterns are starting to form.
Owner Relationship
Out of Alignment
  • Your sense of self-worth is rising and falling with the performance of the business. A good week feels like personal success. A bad week feels like personal failure.
  • You are normalizing overwhelm as the cost of entrepreneurship rather than reading it as a signal that something needs to change.
  • You interpret something not going how you expected as a failure instead of valuable data you can be grateful for.
  • You are taking pride in doing everything yourself in a way that is hardening into your identity. Being the one who does it all is becoming who you are.
  • Early accomplishments are masking the unsustainability of your current pace.
  • Control is feeling like competence. The line between healthy engagement and codependency is invisible to you, which is exactly why this is a high-risk moment for the pattern to set.
  • The volume of decisions and the inability to distinguish the consequential from the trivial has created a baseline pressure you have stopped noticing because it is constant.
  • You are making decisions without a clear definition of what role you want the business to play in your life.
  • What you are building now is becoming the foundation everything else sits on. If it does not match what you want, you will eventually have to slow down or undo it.
In Alignment
  • You are observing without judgment where your energy goes, what you are naturally strong at, what you are afraid of, and what you are doing only because it needs doing.
  • You are naming patterns around money, worth, boundaries, and authority as they show up in the business. You do not need to resolve them. You need to see them.
  • You are recognizing the work that drains you, knowing these are the tasks to hand off first when you reach Stage 2 and 3.
  • You are beginning to distinguish your identity from the business's identity. You can notice when pride, control, or ego are driving decisions and pause before acting on them.
  • You are interpreting unexpected outcomes as data you can learn from, not as evidence of personal failure.
  • You are letting wonder and overwhelm coexist without making either of them mean something about you.
  • You have made conscious decisions about what role you want the business to play in your life, and you are using that as a reference point for what you build.
Stage 2 Readiness
Operational Structure
  • You have minimum viable systems in place. They are not perfect, but they exist outside your head and could be picked up by someone else if needed.
  • Pricing is sustainable and reflects the value of the work, not just what you thought you could charge in the beginning.
  • You have documented your most important processes and decisions in a way that captures both what you do and why.
  • You can see where the natural ceiling of one person operating this business is, and you are starting to think about what comes next.
  • The business has produced enough consistency in revenue and operations that you can begin to look beyond survival.
  • You have identified the tasks and functions that drain you and noted them as the first candidates for handoff in the next stage.
Owner Relationship
  • You have a clear definition of what role you want the business to play in your life, and you are building toward that rather than away from it.
  • You can observe yourself in the business without immediate judgment. You can see your patterns and choose how to respond to them rather than being driven by them.
  • You have begun to distinguish your identity from the business. You know that your worth is not the same as the business's performance.
  • You are open to the next stage and what it will require, even if it feels unfamiliar.
  • You have built enough self-awareness about your patterns around control, authority, money, and worth that you can recognize them when they show up in Stage 2 rather than being surprised by them.
Stage 2
You are the bottleneck
Operational Structure
  • The business has moved past the early survival period. Revenue is consistent enough and the concept is proven.
  • The business is producing more than one person can comfortably handle alone without consequence of overextension or delaying outcomes.
  • The business is starting to demand specialization. Functions that lived together in Stage 1 (sales, delivery, finance, operations) are beginning to require separate attention.
  • You can see the difference between work only you can do and work that is operationally necessary but outside your zone of genius.
  • You are spending meaningful time on the second category and it is starting to cost you opportunities.
  • Minimal systems and processes exist.
  • You are considering bringing on support, or have brought on minimal targeted support such as contractors, fractional roles, or admin.
Owner Relationship
  • You know more opportunity is available but your capacity is fully allocated to maintaining what already exists.
  • The novelty of building something new has faded. You have done this work enough times that it no longer carries the charge it once did.
  • You have a better sense of which tasks and parts of the business drain you and which energize you.
  • The relationship between you and the business is changing as you imagine what it will look like to bring others in.
Operational Structure
Out of Alignment
  • You are the central hub for everything, but the volume has increased to the point where things either slip or are late.
  • You have revenue that should indicate stability but you still feel like the business could collapse if you stepped away for a week.
  • Systems from Stage 1 are patched together rather than intentionally evolved. Every new pressure reveals another gap you did not know was there.
  • You continue to avoid building documentation because the daily urgency of operations always wins, even though you know it is the only way out of the bottleneck.
  • You know you need help but you cannot articulate what role it would be because the work lives tangled inside you rather than separated into definable functions.
  • You have not revisited pricing since Stage 1.
  • The business is tied directly to the time you spend in it rather than to structure that would allow you to leverage that time.
  • You are mistaking revenue growth for readiness to move to the next stage.
  • New opportunities have not landed yet, and the existing work is not structured well enough to hand off. It is too much for you but not yet transferable to someone else.
  • If you have brought on targeted support, you did not set it up with clear scope or ownership. You are now spending time managing them or redoing their work, which has added to your load rather than reduced it.
In Alignment
  • Systems have evolved from personal tools into transferable infrastructure. "How I keep track of things" is becoming "how the business keeps track of things".
  • Key processes are documented well enough that someone unfamiliar with your workflow could follow them.
  • You have clarity on what only you should be doing and what is operationally necessary but should be removed from your role as soon as it is possible.
  • Pricing reflects value and sustainability. Revenue growth is coming from refinement, not just from more volume.
  • The business has enough consistency that you can see patterns in how it operates rather than reacting to what is in front of you.
  • You are intentionally building structure that feels slightly premature, because you know what is coming and you are choosing to build before the pressure arrives.
  • If you have brought on targeted support, you set it up with clear scope and ownership, so it is genuinely reducing your load rather than adding to it.
Owner Relationship
Out of Alignment
  • The exhaustion you feel does not make sense to you. The business is working. You should be happy. You are not.
  • You are reaching for strategies and tactics to feel in control but the thought of implementing them feels like more pressure instead of relief.
  • You do not have capacity for anything beyond maintaining what is critical.
  • You are making decisions based on what is working right now, what is available, or what feels urgent, not based on what you actually want.
  • You have not defined what success looks like for you beyond "more", so every decision adds to the load without clarifying direction.
  • Pride in doing everything yourself is calcifying into identity. 'I am the one who holds this together' is becoming the story, and it is limiting what the business can become.
  • You are saying 'yes' from a place of scarcity, not clarity.
  • Misalignment is beginning to compound. What you take on now becomes harder to unwind later.
  • You are resisting or procrastinating taking actions that will lead to your separation from the business because it feels like loss of control.
In Alignment
  • You understand that the fatigue you are feeling is developmental, not personal. You are not broken. You are in a transition.
  • You have named what you want the business to make possible in your life and you are using that as a filter for decisions.
  • You have examined your relationship with control and you can see where it serves your growth and the business's growth, and where it constrains both.
  • The identity shift from "I am this business" to "I am building this business" is happening. You can hold it without panic.
  • You have built enough energetic surplus that you have capacity to invest in the transition rather than entering it depleted.
  • You are saying yes from clarity rather than scarcity. Opportunities are evaluated against your direction, not your fear of missing out.
  • You can begin separating from the business in small ways without it feeling like loss.
Stage 3 Readiness
Operational Structure
  • Roles or functions needed to expand the business are defined clearly, even if not yet filled. This applies whether you are hiring a person or designing a system to absorb the work.
  • Financial infrastructure supports the cost of bringing someone in without creating survival pressure.
  • You know what you will hand off first and why.
  • You have enough documentation and enough operational clarity that a new person could be onboarded without consuming all of your time.
  • You have a clear picture of what your business looks like in Stage 3 and you have made an intentional choice about whether that includes people, systems, or both.
Owner Relationship
  • You are ready to let someone else touch the business, even if the thought is uncomfortable.
  • You understand that the first person you bring in may not be perfect and that is okay. You are not expecting them to care about the business exactly as you do.
  • You can hold trust and accountability at the same time, rather than collapsing into control or abdication.
  • You are at peace with the fact that the stage you are leaving served you well, and you do not need to recreate its energy in the next stage.
  • You have made a conscious decision about how you want to grow, rather than defaulting to what other businesses look like at this scale.
Stage 3
You operate the business
Operational Structure
  • Other people are involved in the business. Contractors, employees, partners, or some combination.
  • The business requires more hands than yours alone to operate at its current level.
  • Roles exist, even if they are in their first iteration and will need refinement over time.
  • You are managing both the work and the people doing the work, which require different skill sets.
  • Decisions, communication, and direction are flowing through you to the team.
  • The business is producing more than it could when you were the only operator, but coordination, consistency, and accountability now require active attention.
  • The team is large enough that other people can see things about the business you cannot see from inside it.
  • The decisions you are making now are higher-stakes than they were in earlier stages. Hiring, structure, and direction are starting to lock in how the business will operate.
Owner Relationship
  • Your relationship with the work has shifted from doing to leading. You are no longer just executing. You are also responsible for how others execute.
  • The patterns you set in earlier stages are now visible in how you lead. Your defaults around control, trust, communication, and authority are being expressed through the team.
  • You are encountering questions of leadership identity that did not exist when the business was just you.
  • You are starting to be able to define the culture of your business through others, not just theoretically.
  • The way you respond to imperfection in others is reflecting back something about how you hold imperfection in yourself.
  • You are confronting what your role actually is when other people are doing parts of the work.
Operational Structure
Out of Alignment
  • You delegated tasks without transferring the systems, context, or authority needed to execute them, and now you are spending more time correcting and managing than the work would have taken if you did it yourself.
  • You brought people in before processes were defined, and now the breakdowns feel like personnel problems when they are actually structural ones.
  • You are running shadow operations. Quietly redoing, checking, or fixing work that was handed off, which undermines every handoff you make.
  • Roles exist on paper but accountability is unclear. When something fails, no one knows whose responsibility it was, including you.
  • The team depends on you for answers, direction, and decisions at a frequency that recreates the bottleneck you thought you were solving by hiring.
  • Documentation lives in different people's heads or scattered files rather than centralized somewhere the business can access. This perpetuates institutional knowledge and creating risk.
  • You are rejecting approaches that differ from your own, even when they are operationally sound, because they do not match how you would have done it.
  • Hires, systems, and processes are being built reactively without reference to where the business is going, which means the structure you are creating now may not serve the business, or more importantly the life you actually want.
In Alignment
  • You are delegating with full context, authority, and the systems needed to execute. The handoffs are complete and clean.
  • Processes are documented well enough that the people doing the work can operate with autonomy rather than dependent execution.
  • Roles have clear scope, authority, and accountability. When something works or breaks, ownership is obvious.
  • You are leading the team rather than managing every task. Your time is spent on direction, decisions, and the work that only you can do.
  • Communication systems allow information to move through the business without requiring you as the central relay point.
  • Hires, systems, and processes are being built with intention, in service of the business and the life you actually want.
  • Documentation lives within the business in a centralized way, not in any single person's head, including yours.
  • You are receptive to approaches that differ from yours, evaluating them on whether they work rather than whether they match your style.
  • The business can absorb a personnel change without destabilizing.
Owner Relationship
Out of Alignment
  • You are using micromanagement to manage the discomfort of letting go.
  • You are holding onto tasks that should be delegated. Just because you can do them does not mean you should.
  • You are conflating execution with providing the highest value, so you keep doing work that someone else should be doing because it feels like proof of your contribution.
  • You feel threatened by team members whose skills meet or exceed yours in areas you used to own.
  • You are chasing the "at capacity" high. Being maxed out feels like proof you are doing enough.
  • You have not reassessed your definition of success before making higher-impact decisions, so scaling continues to reflect your habits or preferences rather than your intention.
  • You are building and managing relationships with contractors, employees, or partners that the future version of the business cannot sustain.
  • You are taking the team's mistakes personally, which is making it harder for them to learn and harder for you to lead.
In Alignment
  • You have developed a leadership identity that is distinct from your executor identity.
  • You are no longer measuring your value by output.
  • You can hold accountability without micromanagement and trust without disengaging.
  • Delegation feels like expansion, not loss.
  • Mistakes from the team feel like feedback rather than failure, both for them and for you.
  • You have a clear definition of what you want the business and your role within it to become. Every team decision, system, and structural choice is being made in service of that direction.
  • You are protecting your energy for the work that only you should be doing, rather than spreading it across everything.
  • You appreciate team members whose skills meet or exceed yours, recognizing their strength as the business's strength.
  • You can lead through your team's mistakes without taking them personally.

Operational Structure

  • Your relationship with the business is no longer defined by what you do inside it. It is defined by how you lead and steward it from outside the work.
  • You are confronting what your relationship with the business actually is when it no longer needs you to function.
  • The freedom you built toward is now available, and you have to decide what to do with it.
  • This is where control either evolves into leadership, or reasserts itself as interference.
  • Your identity, if it was built on being essential, is being asked to reorganize around something else.

  • The team understands when to involve you and when not to, and the boundary is respected on both sides.

  • You are not resisting the urge to intervene unnecessarily.
  • You confuse facts for feelings. You have a bias against things or people you do not like, and you treat your preferences as if they were objective truths.

  • You have built something successful and you are miserable inside of it, and you cannot figure out why.

  • You are at peace with the business operating without you. You do not need it to need you.
  • You can feel both connected to and separate from the business at the same time.

  • You have redefined what success looks like at this stage of your life, and the business is being shaped to serve that definition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do you focus on the owner's relationship with the business?
Most structural issues start there. How you engage with decisions, control, delegation, and your identity inside the business shapes what it can become. Operational problems typically trace back to a relationship pattern that has not evolved with the stage the business is in, or the one it needs to move into next.
What kinds of problems can I bring to a session?
Anything related to direction, structure, decision making, capacity, growth constraints, or situations where effort and results do not match.
What if I don't know what to focus on or bring?
That is enough to begin. Clarifying the problem is part of the work. Many clients arrive with a general sense that something is not working and leave with clear language for what is happening and what needs to change.
How do you determine what structure my business needs at each stage?
Using the 5-stage framework, I assess operational load, decision distribution, and dependency on the owner. Structure is determined by what the business requires to function at its current stage and what it requires to progress without increasing dependency on you.
Can you help me decide whether to grow, maintain, or scale back?
Yes. That decision is a core part of why I do this. Growth is not the default answer. The right direction depends on capacity, alignment with the life you want, and whether your current structure can actually support what you are considering.
Are sessions in person or virtual?
Virtual. Sessions are held on Google Meet, on camera. If you're in the Minneapolis, MN area and want to meet in person, please reach out to me at scheduling@eighty8vision.com.
Do you offer services beyond the Business Evaluation?
The Business Evaluation is the entry point to all additional services. Ongoing work is customized and offered on a case-by-case basis following the evaluation.
What if I only want help with a specific issue?
The session will still address it, but it will be evaluated in the context of your overall business. Specific issues are rarely isolated from the structure or how you are operating inside it.
Do I need to be at a certain stage or revenue level to work with you?
No. I have experience across all stages. My approach shifts based on where your business is operating and what it needs to move forward. A proactive investment is far less costly than a reactive investment. I work with owners across all stages of revenue and business development.
Is there an accessible pricing option?
Yes. An adjusted rate is available for businesses generating under $100,000 in annual revenue. Availability is limited and reserved for a small number of businesses at any given time.
Can I work with you if I don't have a business yet?
Yes. In my ideal world, I'd be working with owners at the beginning of their journey. Building clarity, direction, and foundational structure early prevents patterns that are harder and more costly to unwind once the business is in motion.
